[17] 2. The Community Pool analogy, brand and business model
[18] The metaphor is the business model, not just the branding.
[19] The problem we are solving. Magic: The Gathering has a steep first step. New players often discover the game through a set, character or universe they already love, but there is very little soft entry between “I’m interested” and “buy a full deck at a premium price, learn a complex set of rules and play-style tactics, then head to your local game store to play, where, even in a friendly setting, you’re still very likely to get badly beaten.” There is no obvious swim school: no simple place to test the waters, learn at a comfortable pace/budget and understand what comes next.
[20] At the other end of the player journey, established players accumulate large amounts of bulk. Most of those cards are still perfectly usable, but they are difficult and inefficient to sell individually. A player can create marketplace listings, build their own store, price every card, manage inventory, pick and pack orders, handle postage and maintain listings, but for most people that is far too much work. The result is that thousands of playable cards simply sit in boxes as dead stock.
[21] The same fragmented market also creates a problem for buyers. Someone may only want a handful of low-value cards worth 25 or 50 cents each, but finding those exact cards can mean buying from several different sellers and paying postage multiple times or going to a premium singles seller like MTGMate where a floor value of $1 is applied to every card. Very quickly, the shipping costs become worth far more than the cards themselves, turning what should have been a cheap purchase into an unreasonable one.
[22] There is also a product gap in the way lower-value cards are used. Commander, currently one of Magic’s most visible formats, generally only allows one copy of each non-basic card, which naturally limits demand for duplicate commons and uncommons. Standard, by contrast, allows up to four copies of the same card and is becoming increasingly visible to newer players, particularly through MTG Arena. Yet ready-to-play Standard decks are poorly represented as an ongoing retail product.
[23] That creates an opportunity. By centralising and turning low-value bulk cards into thoughtfully designed beginner products — such as Pool Party modules (similar to the existing Jumpstart product), casual starter decks and Standard-legal starter decks — Community Pool can give new players an easier way into the game while also creating genuine demand for cards that would otherwise struggle to move.
[24] The result is a model that solves several problems at once: new players get a softer entry into Magic, buyers get access to a broad catalogue through one store and one postage cost, and existing players gain a future pathway for turning unwanted bulk into useful inventory instead of dead stock.
[25] The Community Pool solution. A person who wants to swim does not need to build a pool in their backyard, install filtration, maintain the water, build a slide, hire a swimming teacher and run a snack bar. They can go to the community pool, where the infrastructure already exists and someone else takes care of the maintenance.
[26] TCG Community Pool is the same idea applied directly to trading cards. New players come to one place to test the waters, learn to play and find their next step. Collectors can browse sets. Players can buy singles from one shared catalogue. Later, contributors can place their unwanted cards into the same Pool and let Community Pool handle the difficult work of cataloguing, pricing, storing, selling, fulfilling and tracking them, and the contributor gets paid when their cards move on.
[27] The Pool therefore connects both sides of the same ecosystem: it helps create and support players, then gives those players somewhere useful to send the excess cards they eventually accumulate.
[28] This metaphor should be obvious from the first screen of the website. A user should quickly understand that Community Pool is both a beginner-friendly place to enter the game and, in the long term, shared infrastructure that lets ordinary players sell through an existing system rather than becoming retailers themselves.
[29] Phase 1 business model. At launch, Community Pool sells Jamie's owned Magic inventory only. This is intentionally a live proof of the storefront, products, inventory controls and customer journey. The contributor system is not publicly active yet, but the website should explain the long-term model and the backend should be tested with mock contributors.
[30] Future business model. Once the model is proven, Community Pool becomes the maintained shared pool: contributors provide cards, Community Pool provides the infrastructure and customer base, and creates curated ways to move cards rather than just selling singles, and contributors receive a payout when eligible inventory sells under the agreed FIFO/payout rules.

[62] Purpose. This page sells curated gameplay products and guides a new player from first game through constructed play and eventually organized Standard.
[63] Default product order
[64] 1. Pool Party Pack - first recommended product for two people learning together. Contains 4 x 20-card modules that are interchangeable – combine any two and start to play. Quickest gameplay option.
[65] 2. Pool Party Floaties - add 2 more 20-card modules and expand the number of mix-and-match combinations.
[66] 3. Beginner Casual Deck - Built for casual/kitchen-table play rather than a sanctioned rotating format. Uses normal 60-card constructed conventions but can draw from the wider Community Pool inventory. For those ready to extend gameplay or get more intimate playstyle of a specific color/deck.
[67] 4. Beginner Standard Deck - currently Standard-legal 60-card deck for a player ready to play Standard at a local game store through organized events.
[68] 5. Singles / upgrades - continue improving, personalizing or building new decks.

[128] 4.1 Minimum card/SKU fields
[129] Card name
[130] Set / set code
[131] Collector number
[132] Finish (foil/nonfoil)
[133] Condition
[134] Quantity
[135] Current Community Pool sale price
[136] Standard legality flag where available
[137] Status / availability
[138] Optional owner ID and batch ID fields for contributor simulation
[139] 4.2 Physical SKU vs gameplay identity
[140] Keep these concepts separate. Physical inventory is identified by exact printing/collector number/finish/condition. Gameplay legality and the four-copy rule operate by card name. An older printing can be Standard legal if that same card name currently has a Standard-legal printing.
Additional Notes on Standard Legal: Use Scryfall IDs/card data as the source for card metadata such as current format legality, color, rules text and set metadata. Do not infer Standard legality solely from set age or set name.
[141] 4.3 Curated product inventory
[142] A Beginner Deck, Pool Party module, Floaties pack or Set Availability product must be assembled from live inventory.
[143] When stock is allocated to a curated product, the system must prevent that physical quantity from simultaneously being sold elsewhere.
[144] This is an internal inventory reservation only; contributor FIFO ownership remains separate.
[145] Selling a curated product decrements all component card quantities.
[146] If a component becomes unavailable, the curated product should either become unavailable or require a controlled substitution/rebuild.
[147] 4.4 Pricing
[148] Current reference value is supplied in the inventory export's 'Purchase price' column for Community Pool testing. Jamie refreshes this field before each new export.
[149] Working calculation: reference value x 1.15, then map into the current Community Pool pricing matrix.
[150] Basic lands currently have a working internal/singles value of $0.15 each.
[151] The pricing matrix may evolve; therefore store pricing logic should be configurable rather than embedded across many pages/components.
[153] 5. Future contributor system - Phase 2 target
[154] Purpose of this section. Do not build all of this for Phase 1. Use it to avoid architectural dead ends and to understand what the eventual contributor experience must support.
[155] 5.1 Core ownership model
[156] Multiple contributors can own quantities of the same exact saleable SKU.
[157] Each contribution enters through an intake batch with an intake/receipt date.
[158] FIFO determines which contributor receives the payout when one unit of that SKU sells.
[159] Physical fulfilment and contributor ownership are separate. If one of five identical copies is placed into a curated deck, that does not mean the first FIFO contributor's specific card is 'reserved'.
[160] A contributor only needs to know whether their quantity is still swimming or has sold. Internal reservation state is not inherently contributor-facing.
[161] FIFO should be applied consistently regardless of sale channel: single, deck, Pool Party, Floaties, Set Availability bundle, etc.
[162] 5.2 Eventual contributor portal: My Pool
[163] Recommended customer-facing name: My Pool.
[164] Dashboard
[165] Cards currently swimming
[166] Recent cards sold
[167] Pending payout
[168] Available payout balance
[169] Store-credit balance
[170] Lifetime sales / payout summary
[171] Recent activity
[172] Your Cards
[173] Show all active contributed cards currently swimming.
[174] Search/filter by card, set, batch, FIFO position, age and other useful fields.
[175] Opening a card should show exact SKU, intake batch, intake date, time in Pool, time remaining to next payout tier if applicable, FIFO position and current estimated payout if sold today.
[176] Potential payout must be labelled as an estimate because reference prices and payout tiers can change before sale.
[177] Do not expose other contributors' identities.
[178] Intake Batches
[179] Permanent historical list of each batch received.
[180] Each batch shows received date, intake completion date, submitted/accepted/rejected quantities, active quantity and sold quantity.
[181] Opening a batch shows its full original card list and current status for every line/copy.
[182] Historical batch records should not disappear when cards sell.
[183] Eventually allow download/export of batch receipts/CSV.
[184] Sales & Payouts
[185] Permanent sales ledger.
[186] For each sold card show: exact card/SKU, batch, intake date, sale date, days in Pool when sold, actual sale price, payout rule/tier, payout amount and payout status.
[187] Show how the card sold: Singles Order, Beginner Casual Deck, Beginner Standard Deck, Pool Party, Floaties, Set Availability bundle, etc.
[188] Payout states should ultimately distinguish pending, available, paid and converted-to-credit.
[189] Show total payout balance and available balance.
[190] Minimum cash withdrawal: $5.
[191] Allow store-credit conversion; store credit should be usable directly in the normal shop account.
[192] Account / contributor settings
[193] Same login/account as the ecommerce store.

[201] 5.3 Future auditability

[217] Import the mock four-contributor inventory.
[218] For each exact SKU, order contributor-owned units by intake timestamp.
[219] When one unit of that SKU sells through any channel, allocate the payout to the oldest eligible contributor unit.
[220] Decrease that contributor's active quantity by one and create a sale/payout record.
[221] Do not tie FIFO payout ownership to the specific physical copy picked for fulfilment.
[222] Curated-product reservation can reduce saleable physical stock without changing which contributor is next in FIFO.
[223] Allow Jamie/Will to inspect the resulting contributor balance and transaction history, even if this is an admin-only table rather than a customer portal.

[267] 9. Final instruction to the AI web builder
[268] Build the simplest reliable Phase 1 store that delivers the customer journeys in this brief. Treat the Community Pool analogy as the organizing concept for the entire experience, not as decorative branding. The website should make two promises understandable immediately: new players have a safe place to test the waters and learn, and the long-term system gives card owners a shared storefront/infrastructure so they do not need to become retailers themselves. Treat the inventory files as live business data, not decorative demo content. Do not invent additional major product categories or contributor features without a clear requirement. Keep code/data structures extensible enough to add contributor ownership, FIFO and payouts later, but prioritise a functioning owned-stock ecommerce launch and a clear new-player experience.
